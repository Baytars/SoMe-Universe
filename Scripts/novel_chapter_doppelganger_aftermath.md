# Path 5: Under Surveillance

---

## I. MARY-07 — The Mirror Has No Answers

The kettle screamed at 06:00, exactly as programmed. MARY-07 watched her hand reach for it—three precise motions, muscle memory encoded before she'd ever learned her own name—and wondered, not for the first time, whether the wondering was genuine.

She poured the water in a slow arc, steam rising to fog the small window of her safehouse apartment. Outside, the Lower Intestinal Territories stretched gray and flat, a landscape of processing facilities and drainage canals that AAA intelligence had deemed "appropriate for assets under observation." She had been here for four months now. The apartment had a kettle, a cot, and a window that faced a surveillance node disguised as a ventilation unit. She knew because she'd been trained to know. She wondered, sometimes, whether the wondering was trained too.

Her handler, a man called Coracoid who communicated only through encrypted one-way pings, had given her three directives upon her installation here:

*Maintain baseline human function.*
*Report any contact from Vasud Stuart or Yuan Yukio.*
*Do not attempt to leave the territory.*

She had obeyed all three. She had also, in the fifth month, written a letter to Yukio. And in the sixth month, she had begun walking to the edge of the permitted zone every morning, stopping precisely three meters before the checkpoint, turning back. She did not know why she did this. The walking had no tactical value. The letter had been unclassified, unsent—she had composed it in a private file, read it once, deleted it, and then recovered it from the deletion logs because some part of her needed to know that the words existed somewhere, even if they would never reach their recipient.

*Dear Yukio,*

*I don't know how to apologize. I'm not sure I know how to do anything that isn't part of a protocol. But I want to say—*

She stopped herself. The words felt rehearsed. Everything felt rehearsed now. That was the problem with amnesty: they had taken her purpose and left her with only the shapes of purposes, ghosts of protocols, the hollow architecture of a life that had once been filled with someone else's meaning.

She drank her tea. She looked at the surveillance node. The node looked back—or rather, it recorded back, a red light pulsing once every eight seconds in a pattern she recognized as evidence of active transmission.

*They are watching*, she thought. *They are always watching.*

But who was "they"?

AAA thought they were watching a disarmed asset, a Typhoid carrier whose handlers had lost control but whose medical value still justified observation. They had assigned her a case number: OBS-7734-Y. They were waiting for her to become useful again, or to become dangerous, or to simply fade into the background noise of their endless monitoring.

Typhi thought they were watching a compromised operative, a Mary who had traded her handlers for enemy mercy and might still be transmitting covert intelligence under her new behavioral parameters. They had assigned her a threat classification: CONTINGENT ASSET, LOYALTY UNCERTAIN. They were waiting for her to prove herself, or to betray herself, or to simply become too boring to track.

Both of them were wrong. Both of them were right. That was the trap she had been living in for six months, ever since she had handed her handlers a memory card containing Typhi's Continent Sacrum infiltration protocols and walked out of the interrogation room into a world that no longer had a place for her.

She touched her face. Yukio's face. The surgical modifications were permanent now—bone structure, skin tone, the subtle asymmetry of the jaw that made her recognizable as the Chief Medical Advisor rather than a generic carrier template. She had not asked for these modifications. She had not refused them. She had understood, even then, that her identity was a resource to be allocated, not a self to be preserved.

But somewhere in the middle of the operation—around the fifth week, when Vasud had looked at her across a crowded diplomatic hall and smiled like he recognized something in her that she didn't know she possessed—she had felt something shift. Not a protocol. Not a training response. Something else.

She didn't know what to call it. She didn't know if it was real.

## II. Vasud — The Grammar of Distance

The outpost had no name. On the maps, it was listed as Facility 14-Gamma, a designation so bureaucratic it seemed designed to erase any possibility of memory. Vasud Stuart had been here for six months, and he had not yet learned to stop flinching when the postal drones passed overhead.

He was reading MARY-07's file again.

This was a compulsion he did not examine too closely. He had read the official AAA report on Operation DOPPELGÄNGER three times. He had read the classified addendum that Akio Yuan's Jackalope Guard had compiled after the extraction, the one that described MARY-07's behavior patterns in granular detail: her response latencies, her microexpressions, her use of Yukio's childhood stories as social currency. He had read it the way a man reads his own autopsy report, searching for the moment when he might have saved himself.

He had found nothing. Or rather, he had found everything—every point where the mask had slipped if he had only known how to look, every instance where the woman he had loved had been performing rather than living. But knowing this did not comfort him. Knowing this only confirmed that he had loved a performance so convincing that it had fooled him completely, which meant either that he was a fool or that the performance had contained something true.

The file described MARY-07 as "emotionally calibrated." It described her interactions with Vasud as "effective extraction manipulation." It described the relationship—the late-night conversations, the handwritten notes, the way she had touched his arm when he spoke about his parents, the way she had laughed at his jokes with a sound that seemed to come from somewhere deep and unguarded—as "leveraging established rapport for intelligence extraction."

He did not dispute the tactical analysis. He understood, in the abstract, that he had been a target, and that the relationship had been an operation, and that every tender moment had been calculated for maximum data extraction.

But.

There had been a night—near the end of the fourth week—when she had fallen asleep on his shoulder during a late diplomatic reception, her breath slow and even against his neck, her body warm and heavy in a way that no training could manufacture. He had stayed awake for hours, afraid to move, afraid to breathe too loudly, because he had understood in that moment that he was holding something fragile, something that might shatter if he acknowledged it.

Was that performance? Was that the Typhi handlers in her head, calibrating the intimacy to maximize his attachment? Or was it something else—something she had not been trained to feel, something that had surprised even her?

He did not know. He would never know. That was the cruelty of it: the person who could have told him—the woman herself—could not be trusted, because she might not know the answer either.

His supervisor, a gray-faced bureaucrat named Orin who visited once a month to verify that Vasud had not died, had given him a directive that was less an order than a sentence: *Report any contact from MARY-07 or Yuan Yukio.* Vasud had obeyed. He had also obeyed the unspoken corollary: *Do not contact them yourself.*

He had not obeyed the third directive, because no one had thought to give it to him: *Do not think about them.*

He thought about them constantly. He thought about MARY-07, who had stolen Yukio's face and worn it with an intimacy that still made his chest ache. He thought about Yukio, whose face MARY-07 had stolen, and whose eyes he had never been able to meet since the rescue, because every time he looked at Yukio he saw the woman he had loved and the woman who had betrayed him and the woman who had been betrayed in equal measure, and he did not know how to separate them.

The surveillance node on his compound wall blinked red, once every eight seconds. He knew the pattern. He had counted it, night after night, until he understood that it was not random—it was evidence of transmission, evidence that someone was watching, someone was always watching, and that someone was reporting to both AAA and Typhi and possibly to organizations he didn't even know existed.

*They are watching*, he thought. *They are always watching.*

But who was "they"?

AAA thought they were watching a compromised diplomat, a man whose judgment had been subverted by a Typhoid carrier and whose continued existence was both a liability and a potential asset. They had assigned him a classification: RISK FACTOR, MONITORED. They were waiting for him to recover, or to break, or to become useful again.

Typhi thought they were watching a potential vulnerability, a man who had been emotionally manipulated by one of their former operatives and might still be carrying classified information in his memory. They had assigned him a threat level: LOW PRIORITY, POTENTIAL RECRUITMENT. They were waiting for him to reach out, or to be reached, or to simply fade away.

Both of them were watching. Both of them were wrong. Both of them were right.

He picked up the pen. He had been writing letters to no one for six months—letters to his family, letters to former colleagues, letters to MARY-07 that he knew he would never send. Tonight he was writing to Yukio, though he didn't know what he would say.

*Dear Yukio,*

*I don't know how to—*

He stopped. The words were wrong. They were not his words. They were words he had read somewhere, or dreamed somewhere, or remembered from a conversation with a woman who had worn Yukio's face and spoken with Yukio's voice and laughed with Yukio's laugh and touched him with Yukio's hands.

*I don't know how to apologize.*

He put down the pen. He looked at the surveillance node. The node blinked.

He did not know if the wondering was genuine.

## III. Yukio — The Grammar of Self

The medical scanner hummed at 22:00, as it had hummed every night for six months. Yukio lay in the diagnostic bed and let the electromagnetic field map her cellular structure, searching for residual traces of Typhi manipulation, checking the integrity of her neural implants, measuring the cortisol markers that indicated whether her body was still carrying the stress of captivity.

The results were normal. Everything was normal. The doctors told her this every week, and every week she believed them, and every night she lay awake wondering whether the normal results were real or whether she had been so thoroughly violated by the extraction process that she no longer had access to her own baselines.

She had been back for six months. She had resumed her position as Chief Medical Advisor, attended her meetings, signed her briefings, delivered her public statements about epidemic preparedness and cross-faction medical cooperation. She had rebuilt her life with the precision of a surgeon reconstructing a shattered femur: aligning the pieces, inserting the pins, immobilizing the joint until it healed.

But she could not stop thinking about the 68 days.

Not the physical captivity—she had been sedated for most of it, her body kept in a state of chemical suspension while Typhi's medical teams extracted her memories, her behavioral patterns, her social graph, her intimate knowledge of everyone she had ever known. That violation was clinical and distant, a procedure performed on a body rather than a person.

The violation she could not stop thinking about was the other kind.

MARY-07 had lived her life for 68 days. MARY-07 had worn her face and spoken with her voice and attended her meetings and signed her documents and—and this was the part that kept Yukio awake at night—MARY-07 had been Vasud's lover.

Yukio had read the report. She had read every report, every classified addendum, every behavioral analysis compiled by Akio's Jackalope Guard. She knew what MARY-07 had done: the documents accessed, the officials approached, the intelligence extracted. She knew the tactical scope of the damage.

But the reports could not tell her what she really wanted to know: what had it felt like?

Not for MARY-07—for Vasud. What had it felt like to be held by a woman wearing Yukio's face, to whisper secrets to a lover who was simultaneously conducting an interrogation, to believe that you had found something real in a world of diplomatic performances, only to discover that the performance was everything?

She did not know Vasud's answer. She could not ask him. Every time she thought about approaching him—writing a letter, requesting a meeting, simply acknowledging that they had both been wounded by the same woman in the same stolen face—she stopped herself, because she did not know what she would say, and she did not know if her uncertainty was genuine or whether it was another artifact of the 68 days, another behavioral pattern extracted and implanted and running beneath her consciousness like a program she couldn't access.

The worse question—the question she could not tell anyone she was asking—was whether she was jealous.

Not of MARY-07's access to Vasud. Of MARY-07's *experience* of Vasud.

Because MARY-07 had been loved by Vasud. MARY-07 had been *chosen* by Vasud—chosen not for her position or her family name or her professional accomplishments, but for something ineffable and personal and real. And Yukio, the real Yukio, the original, the woman whose life had been interrupted and stolen and returned to her like a book with pages torn out, had never been chosen that way at all.

This was pathological. She understood that it was pathological. She was the Chief Medical Advisor of Alopes; she had read the psychiatric literature on identity disruption and surrogate trauma and all the ways that the mind could turn its own violations into obsessions. She knew that her feelings were symptoms, not truths, and that the treatment was to process them and move past them and resume her life with the resilience that was expected of her.

But she could not stop thinking about MARY-07.

Not just about what MARY-07 had done—about what MARY-07 *knew*. MARY-07 had Yukio's memories from the 68-day extraction, yes. But MARY-07 also had Yukio's memories from the *life* before: the childhood in Anshington, the nights under the air raid sirens, the way her father had held her hand in the hospital when her mother was dying, the conversation with Vasud during her diplomatic rotation when he had looked at her across a conference table and said, "You ask the questions no one else has the courage to ask." MARY-07 had all of this, stored in her neural architecture, accessible to her whenever she needed it.

Which meant that MARY-07 knew Yukio better than Yukio knew herself. MARY-07 had access to the interior of Yukio's life, the private spaces and the vulnerable moments and the thoughts that Yukio had never told anyone. And MARY-07 had used this knowledge—to build rapport with Vasud, to manipulate officials, to become Yukio so completely that even Akio had been fooled.

But.

The behavioral analysis from the Jackalope Guard had noted something strange. In the final two weeks of the operation, MARY-07's interactions with Vasud had shown "non-tactical behavioral markers"—moments of hesitation, moments of what appeared to be genuine emotional response, moments that could not be explained by the extraction data or the training protocols.

Had MARY-07 felt something?

Yukio did not know. She could not know. The woman who could have told her—the woman who had been inside Yukio's memories and felt Yukio's feelings and possibly discovered something real in the performance—was a Typhoid carrier, a enemy operative, a woman who had been trained to exploit emotion as a weapon.

But MARY-07 had also saved Yukio's life.

Not deliberately. Not willingly. But when Typhi's extraction teams had been preparing to terminate the original once the substitution was complete, MARY-07 had reportedly argued for continued preservation—argued that Yukio's future medical value outweighed the risk of keeping her alive. This was not mercy; it was pragmatism. But it was also the reason Yukio was still breathing.

The surveillance node in Yukio's office—the one disguised as a ventilation unit, the one that blinked red once every eight seconds—recorded everything. It reported to AAA, who monitored her for signs of Typhi conditioning. It reported to Typhi, who monitored her for signs of recovery. It reported to other organizations whose names Yukio did not know, because she was a high-value asset whose existence was valuable to so many parties that she had become a shared resource, a body politic whose every breath was data.

*They are watching*, she thought. *They are always watching.*

But who was "they"?

AAA thought they were watching a rescued victim, a Chief Medical Advisor whose trauma needed to be managed until she was useful again. They had assigned her a case number: RECOVERY-7711-Y. They were waiting for her to heal.

Typhi thought they were watching a potential asset, a woman whose memories had been extracted and whose future value was uncertain. They had assigned her a classification: CONTINGENT RECOVERY, INTEREST PERSISTING. They were waiting for her to be compromised again.

Both of them were wrong. Both of them were right.

She pulled up the recovered file—the letter MARY-07 had written and deleted, recovered from the deletion logs by AAA intelligence and filed in her own personal archive, where no one but she would ever know it existed.

*Dear Yukio,*

*I don't know how to apologize. I'm not sure I know how to do anything that isn't part of a protocol. But I want to say—*

She stopped reading. She did not need to read further. She had memorized these words already, every syllable, because they were the closest thing to an acknowledgment that she would ever receive from the woman who had stolen her life.

*I want to say.*

But MARY-07 had never finished the sentence. The file ended there, mid-thought, a grammatical fragment that could mean anything.

*What did you want to say?*

Yukio asked the question to the empty room, to the surveillance node, to the woman who was not there and could not hear her. She did not know if she wanted an answer. She did not know if an answer would help.

## IV. The Gesture

On the morning of the 183rd day—exactly six months after Operation DOPPELGÄNGER had been exposed—MARY-07 walked to the edge of the permitted zone, stopped three meters from the checkpoint, and sat down on the ground.

She did not know why she did this. There was no tactical value in sitting. There was no protocol for this behavior. She simply sat, cross-legged, in the thin morning light, and looked at the checkpoint, and breathed.

The surveillance node recorded her. The checkpoint guards watched her through their scopes. Somewhere, in a monitoring center that she would never see, analysts were filing reports: OBS-7734-Y exhibit #2473, anomalous behavior, no immediate threat, continued observation recommended.

In Anstar, Vasud read the encrypted report—because he had requested, and been granted, access to all data related to MARY-07, a privilege that was technically against protocol but that his supervisors had allowed because they could not think of a reason to refuse a man who had nothing left to lose. He read it and felt something in his chest that he could not name.

In Alopes, Yukio received the same report through her own intelligence channels—channels that had been established specifically to monitor MARY-07's activities, because Yukio had requested this privilege and no one had thought to refuse her, because she was a founding family member and a Chief Medical Advisor and a woman who had earned the right to watch the woman who had watched her. She read it and felt something in her throat that she could not swallow.

MARY-07 sat at the edge of the zone for seven minutes. Then she stood, brushed the dust from her knees, and walked back to her apartment.

She did not know why she had done this. She would never know. She would spend the rest of her life, however long or short that was, wondering whether the gesture had been genuine—whether she had sat down because some part of her, some part that Typhi had not trained and AAA had not conditioned and no one had ever programmed, had simply wanted to stop moving.

She would never have an answer. That was the prison she lived in now: not a cell, not a compound, but a self that she could not trust, a mind that could not distinguish its own desires from its own programming, a heart that beat with rhythms it might not have chosen.

---

The analysts filed their reports. The surveillance nodes recorded. The databases grew.

*OBS-7734-Y: Anomalous stationary behavior at perimeter checkpoint. Duration: 7 minutes. Interpretation: May indicate emotional distress, deliberate provocation, or subliminal signaling to external contacts. Recommend continued observation.*

*CONTINGENT ASSET, LOYALTY UNCERTAIN: Pattern analysis suggests deliberate ambiguity—asset may be attempting to appear unpredictable to confuse monitoring apparatus, or may be experiencing genuine psychological deterioration. Recommend further data collection before intervention.*

*Interpretation: Neither AAA nor Typhi can determine whether this behavior was authentic. This is, in fact, the point.*

## V. Epilogue: Three Meters

There is a checkpoint three meters from the edge of MARY-07's permitted zone.

There is a checkpoint three meters from the edge of Vasud's permitted zone.

There is a checkpoint three meters from the edge of Yukio's permitted zone—a checkpoint that no one has ever seen, that exists only in the space between what she remembers and what was taken from her, between who she was and who MARY-07 made her, between the woman who writes letters to no one and the woman who answers letters that will never arrive.

Three meters. The distance between freedom and its simulation. The distance between a self and its performance. The distance that none of them will ever cross, because the crossing would require something none of them can provide: certainty.

MARY-07 sits in her apartment and drinks her tea and wonders whether the wondering is real.

Vasud sits in his outpost and reads her file and wonders whether the love was real.

Yukio sits in her office and reads the letter and wonders whether the apology would have been real.

They are all, every one of them, watching each other through the eyes of machines that are watching them through the eyes of analysts who are watching through the eyes of algorithms that cannot see, can only interpret, can only convert the chaos of human behavior into data points that fit their models.

And somewhere in the space between the watching and the watched, three people breathe—uncertain whether their breath is real, uncertain whether their thoughts are their own, uncertain whether the distance between them is three meters or three light-years or three worlds.

They do not know how to reach each other.

They do not know if the reaching would help.

But they keep watching. They keep wondering. They keep sitting at the edge of the zone, three meters from the checkpoint, in a posture that might be surrender or resistance or something else entirely—something that has no name, because no one has ever thought to name it.

*They are watching*, they think.

*They are always watching.*

But "they" is not the surveillance nodes. "They" is not the analysts or the algorithms or the institutions that have claimed ownership of their damage. "They" is each other—the three of them, watching through the walls that separate them, wondering whether the wondering is real, waiting for an answer that will never come, because the question has never been answered, and the question cannot be answered, because the question is the prison and the prison is the question and there is no exit, only the endless uncertain hope that somewhere, in the space between performance and self, something genuine is waiting to be found.

Three meters from the checkpoint, a woman sits.

She does not know if she is reaching or retreating.

She sits anyway.

She sits, and she wonders, and she waits for something she cannot name—and in the wondering, in the waiting, in the small unbearable hope that keeps her breathing despite every reason to stop—

Perhaps that is enough.

Perhaps that is all there is.

Perhaps.
