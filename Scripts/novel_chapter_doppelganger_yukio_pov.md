# Chapter: The Labyrinth of One's Own Skin

## SoMe Universe — Yukio POV: The 68 Days

---

### Day 1 (2343.03.12)

The sedatives used a benzotropine analog — a tertiary amine crossing the blood-brain barrier within 4.7 minutes of intramuscular injection. I know this because when I regained consciousness, my left deltoid still ached with the precise injection-site tenderness consistent with a 23-gauge needle held in place for the required 10 seconds to prevent retrograde flow. Whoever had taken me was professionally competent. That was the first thing I catalogued. Not fear. Not confusion. Competence.

I woke in a sealed chamber approximately 4.2 meters by 3.1 meters. The walls were polymer composite — sound-dampening, which told me the facility was underground. The air carried the faint ozonic signature of a recycled atmosphere system, consistent with a sealed underground installation. No windows. One door: steel, magnetic lock, no interior handle. A camera dome in the upper northwest corner, wide-angle lens, no visible infrared emitters — night-capable but passive. A cot. A sanitation unit. A small desk bolted to the floor. Fluorescent lighting, 5000 Kelvin color temperature, 24-hour cycle. I could work with this.

My scientific mind began its analysis automatically, the way a cardiologist might notice an irregular pulse in a film she wasn't meant to be reviewing. I noted the exact time from the wall display: 0347. The date stamp confirmed what I suspected — March 12, 2343. The window between my last memory (leaving the lab at Kyoto Meridian Medical at 2215, March 11) and now was approximately 8 hours. Sufficient time for ground transport within a 400-kilometer radius, or 40 minutes by light aircraft. Given the absence of turbulence sickness upon waking, ground transport was more likely. The magnetic lock power draw suggested an independent power grid — not connected to a municipal system.

I had been dreaming before waking. In the dream, I was standing in my own office, wearing my own clothes, giving instructions to my own staff — and no one could see that I was screaming. I had read about this phenomenon in papers on dissociative fugue states. I had never expected to live it.

My captors had made one critical error. They had not searched me thoroughly enough to remove the subdermal micro-identification chip embedded in the dorsal surface of my left wrist. Standard issue for Meridian Medical research staff — a passive RFID tag transmitting my employee credential and biometric hash at 125 kHz. I could use it to mark time. A double-tap against the chip housing registered a session event in the Meridian network. The system would log a communication gap — not my location, but enough to establish that I was alive and unresponsive starting at approximately 0348, March 12.

I tapped it twice. Then I lay back on the cot and began counting the seconds between the camera dome's mechanical adjustment whirs. The camera had a preset sweep pattern: 180-degree arc, 47-second cycle, 12-second pause at each end. I noted this. I noted everything.

The first guard came at 0600. Male, mid-30s, East Asian morphology consistent with Northern Pacific regional ancestry. No insignia. Standard facility uniform — gray polycotton. His gait told me he was not military. Military gait is controlled, energy-conserving, weight slightly forward. His was slightly heavy on the heels. Security contractor. Private sector. The name tag read "Sato." He placed a tray through a lower hatch in the door — rice, miso, water. Nutrient-compromised but calorically adequate.

"Is there a medical reason I'm here?" I asked. My voice was steady. I had practiced this.

He looked at me for 2.3 seconds longer than comfortable. "I'll pass it on."

He did not answer the question. I noted this too.

### Day 7 (2343.03.19)

The second guard was a woman — "Tanaka" — who made a mistake on Day 7. She spoke to Sato at the door, unaware that the acoustic panels, while dampening sound from outside, also concentrated sound from the corridor within the cell. She thought the cell was acoustically isolated. She was wrong.

"The replacement's handling Vasud's file well. Corporate's impressed."

Sato's response was low, but I caught: "— not our problem. Typhi's people are running it."

I lay on my cot in the dark and processed this. "Replacement." A replacement was not a hostage. A hostage is held for leverage. A replacement is held as a substrate — while something else occupies your life. Vasud. The name meant nothing to me at that moment. I did not yet know that Dr. Vasud was my former teaching assistant at Kyoto Meridian, or that someone was impersonating me at work.

The critical data gap was context. I filed the fragments: replacement, Vasud, Typhi, corporate. The scientific method demands patience. You observe. You collect. You do not theorize prematurely.

### Day 12 (2343.03.24)

I began sketching the facility from sound.

The HVAC system operated on a zone-based circulation model — I could hear the variable-air-volume diffuser cycling in adjacent corridors. The rhythm was consistent with a standard underground research installation, likely one of the decommissioned seismic shelters repurposed after the 2331 Pacific Rim infrastructure modernization. The zone schedule suggested three distinct operational areas: administrative (0600-2200 cycle), laboratory (continuous), and residential (irregular, suggesting rotating shift personnel).

The magnetic lock on my door produced a unique harmonic signature when cycled — a 12-microsecond electromagnetic pulse that, while too weak to detect without instruments, created a faint residual magnetism in the door frame. I mapped this by touch. The door was not on the perimeter wall. I was in an interior chamber — a detention cell within a larger facility, not a standalone black site. This changed the threat calculus. A standalone site is self-contained. An interior chamber within an active facility means traffic. People. Human error.

I refined my map. Cell 7, sublevel 2, block unknown. I scratched these coordinates into the underside of the desk bolt with my thumbnail, using a system of pressure marks that encoded the information in a binary notation only I could read.

### Day 17 (2343.03.29)

Day 17 was the day I began to understand what I was.

The third guard — "Yamamoto," a nervous man who looked at me with something uncomfortably close to pity — left the door hatch open for 11 seconds while exchanging words with someone in the corridor. In that window, I heard a PA announcement:

*"Dr. Tanaka, Pathology, please report to Lab 3-East. Dr. Tanaka, Pathology."*

And then, moments later, another voice on the same PA:

*"Dr. Yukio, Conference Room A is ready for your briefing."*

I stood motionless at the center of my cell.

Dr. Yukio. That was me. I was Dr. Yukio. I was standing in a sealed room, wearing a facility-issued jumpsuit, on Day 17 of my captivity. And Dr. Yukio was somewhere else, being paged to a conference room.

The implications organized themselves in my mind with the clinical efficiency of a diagnostic algorithm:

1. A person with my credentials — my identity — was functioning in my professional environment.
2. This person was not me, because I was here.
3. Therefore, my identity had been duplicated. Stolen. Assumed.
4. The theft was operational, not incidental. Whoever was operating as Dr. Yukio had access to my biometric credentials, my professional network, and presumably my behavioral patterns.

I sat on the cot and performed a controlled breathing exercise. Four counts in. Seven counts hold. Eight counts out. I had read about this technique in a paper on stress-induced cognitive degradation in isolated research subjects. It was effective. My mind remained clear.

"Replacement" was no longer the operative word. The operative word was *proxy*. Someone was living my life as a sustained intelligence operation. And I needed to understand the operation's parameters before I could understand its weaknesses.

### Day 21 (2343.04.02)

The next significant intelligence fragment came through the door hatch again. Two guards — Sato and a new man I categorized as "Nakamura" based on the nameplate — were discussing shift schedules.

Nakamura: "— Vasud's been meeting with her. The real Yukio's old TA. She's pulling him in nicely."

Sato: "Typhi wants it slow. Long game."

Nakamura: "Guy doesn't suspect?"

Sato: "She looks exactly like her. Acts like her. Hell, she *is* her, as far as his neural pattern-matching goes."

I catalogued this with surgical precision. Vasud — my former preceptor. Yes. I remembered him now: Vasud Stuart, the senior diplomat who had supervised my rotation through the Ministry of Foreign Affairs years ago. He had exceptional pattern recognition and limited social intuition — easy to read, easy to mislead. He had asked me questions about biological warfare that no other diplomat had thought to ask, which I later realized was precisely why Typhi chose him: not despite his analytical brilliance, but because of it.

And someone — this doppelganger — was running a long-game operation targeting him. Typhi. The name had surfaced before. A corporate intelligence contractor? A government proxy? I needed to know.

But the critical insight was not emotional. I did not feel betrayal. I did not feel anger on Vasud's behalf. I felt something far more useful: clarity. If Typhi was running a long game on Vasud, Vasud was not the target. He was the vector. Someone was using Vasud to access something through him — information, institutional leverage, a chain of connections. The question was: what did Vasud have that merited a two-month operation, a trained replacement, and a secure underground facility?

I began cross-referencing. Vasud had transitioned from Meridian to a role at the Anstar Ministry of Foreign Affairs six months ago — a lateral move that seemed puzzling until I considered the access profile. Ministry positions had institutional access to diplomatic cables, intelligence assessments, and — critically — internal evaluations of Anstar's own military establishments. If Typhi's doppelganger was using me-as-proxy to cultivate Vasud, the value was institutional intelligence: Anstar's assessment of its own power structure, its factional fault lines, its internal vulnerabilities. The Vasud angle was not about pharmaceutical pipelines. It was about mapping the trust architecture of a state.

This was not personal. It was a reconnaissance operation against Anstar's own nervous system.

I noted this in my mental model and continued listening.

### Day 31 (2343.04.12)

The guards had become complacent. It had taken 31 days, but Sato had started哼歌 in the corridor — a tune I recognized as a pre-Consolidation pop song, which placed his cultural baseline in the late 2320s. Complacency made people verbose. On Day 31, he was on comms with someone in a different block:

"— Shire's gala was last night. You see the feeds? Shire family, Robert Shire, and this Yukio woman — must be a different one from ours—"

He stopped. Lowered his voice. But I had heard enough.

Shire. Robert Shire. The Shire family commanded the Surgical Naval Marines — one of the most powerful military institutions in the Continental Sacrum alliance, controlling surgical strike capability, field medical operations, and the joint medical-military affairs portfolio across the Federation's eastern territories. The family had built its identity on aggressive medical-military integration: the human body as a battlefield, surgery as a form of warfare. A marriage alliance with Shire would embed a foreign national at the intersection of military command and medical intelligence — access that no amount of corporate espionage could replicate.

Someone was marrying me to the heir of Anstar's most powerful military dynasty.

I processed this in layers. Layer one: my face, my name, my identity were being used to secure a marriage alliance with one of the most powerful families in the Federation. Layer two: Typhi — whoever they were — had orchestrated this in 68 days, which meant the doppelganger operation was not a subsidiary action. It was the primary operation. The Vasud angle was secondary — a utility extraction, not the end goal. Layer three: whoever was running this operation had access to enough capital, intelligence infrastructure, and biometric fabrication technology to create a human-quality doppelganger. That was not a startup. That was a state-level actor or a very sophisticated private military operation.

The Shire alliance was the payload. I was the delivery mechanism, without my knowledge or consent.

I catalogued the date. April 22. The Shire gala had already occurred. I noted the timeline discrepancy — by the time I had begun to understand the operation's scope, it was already substantially completed. The doppelganger had my face at a Shire family event. She had agreed to a marriage proposal in my name. She was negotiating — with my face, my voice, my legal identity — the consolidation of bioethical and pharmaceutical power in a configuration that served Typhi's interests.

And I was in a room with a bolted desk, counting camera sweep cycles.

### Day 42 (2343.04.23)

On Day 42, I received my first visitor who was not a guard.

Dr. Lin. Taiwanese-accented English, age indeterminate, white coat over facility uniform. She carried a medical scanner — I recognized the Meridian R-7 Multi-Diagnostic Platform, a device used for regenerative tissue assessment. She scanned my vitals, took blood (17 milliliters, two vials, no verbal confirmation of what the blood was for), and noted something on a datapad.

"You're healthy," she said. "Better than expected."

"I'm a regenerative medicine researcher," I replied. "I understand cellular stress response."

She looked at me for a moment. Something shifted in her expression — not sympathy, but recognition. One scientist to another.

"The facility director will see you tomorrow," she said. "Dress accordingly."

I had not known there was a facility director. I had not known there was a plan for me beyond indefinite detention. The announcement of a meeting with the director suggested one of two things: the operation was entering a new phase, or they were preparing to dispose of me and wanted to assess my remaining utility before doing so.

Either way, I needed information. I needed leverage. And I needed to understand what Typhi's leadership was prepared to offer me.

The meeting, when it came on Day 43, was brief. The director was a small man, impeccably groomed, with the compressed intensity of someone who had learned to see people as variables in an equation. He sat across from me in the visitation chamber — my first time out of the cell in six weeks — and placed a document on the table between us. I recognized him from open-source medical intelligence briefings: a senior Typhi operations commander, possibly with formal Umin command authority over the facility. He was not someone whose name appeared in public documents. But I had read his published work on regenerative tissue applications in espionage contexts. He was, in the neutral language of academic assessment, brilliant and utterly without conscience.

It was a medical report. My medical report. The doppelganger's vitals, genetic markers, and neurological profile, generated by the R-7 scanner I had helped design. The doppelganger was not biologically me. The medical data told a different story — one Typhi's director had apparently decided I was sophisticated enough to understand and accept. The carrier template used for MARY-07 was a human being: a Salmonella Typhi asymptomatic carrier identified through the Typhi Division's screening database, selected for 94% pre-operative physical similarity to my baseline. Four weeks of surgical refinement, hormonal conditioning, and superficial gene therapy had closed the remaining gap. The tissue work was extraordinary — not regenerative growth from my template, but modification of existing human biology to approximate mine.

I said this aloud, and I meant it as a scientific observation, but the director interpreted it as a compliment and smiled.

"She's our finest work," he said. "MARY-07. We call her that internally. MARY. Medical Anthropological ReplacemenY." He pronounced it with satisfaction. "Seventeen iterations. She's the first to pass Turing-adjacent criteria in sustained field deployment."

I looked at the medical data. The surgical precision alone represented a decade's worth of advancement beyond anything published in the open literature. The doppelganger — MARY-07 — had been built from a human carrier, modified to approximate my physical parameters through surgical and hormonal intervention, with neural conditioning trained on 847 hours of my recorded behavior from the six months prior to my abduction. She was not a robot. She was not a clone. She was a trained operative wearing modified human biology — a person whose original identity had been overwritten by Typhi's selection protocols, whose gall bladder still carried the Salmonella Typhi that made her valuable as an intelligence asset.

"You modified a human being to look like me," I said.

"We modified a human being *using* you," the director corrected. "There's a distinction."

I looked at the blood work. I looked at the conditioning protocol specifications. I looked at the timeline — February 1 authorization, February 15 training commenced, March 12 I was taken, March 15 MARY-07 deployed. Sixty-eight days of overlap between my capture and her operational debut.

"You have her operating in Shire family circles," I said. "She's agreed to marry Robert Shire."

The director's smile did not waver. "She has agreed to a great many things."

I set the document down. "You're going to use the marriage to position a foreign national inside Anstar's military command structure," I said. "Shire controls surgical strike capability and joint medical-military affairs. With the Vasud pipeline feeding Anstar's internal diplomatic assessments and the Shire marriage opening military-intelligence access, you've built a complete architecture for infiltrating Anstar's most sensitive institutional layers — from the inside, using their own alliance framework as the delivery vehicle."

"Around one *template*," the director said. "You should be proud. Your genetic profile, your professional credentials, your social network — they constitute one of the most strategically valuable identities in the Federation's biomedical sector. We simply realized that before you did."

I said nothing. The director interpreted my silence as defeat. He stood, collected the document, and walked out.

He was wrong about the silence. I was silent because I was recalculating. The doppelganger was not just an impersonation. She was a platform. The director had not created a spy. He had created a *person* — a person with my identity, my credentials, my face, and a neural conditioning protocol that could be updated with new behavioral training. She was being deployed not as a static replacement but as an adaptive agent. She would learn. She would grow. She would become more me with every passing day.

The scariest thought was not that MARY-07 was out there living my life. The scariest thought was that MARY-07 was out there *becoming* my life, and that the distance between what she was and what I was would narrow with every passing week.

### Day 56 (2343.05.07)

I began teaching myself from the fragments.

Without access to outside information — no comms, no data network, no reading material beyond the facility meal tray wrappers — I reconstructed the timeline through guard conversations and the fragments of external audio that leaked through the ventilation system.

By Day 56, I had confirmed: Lonicera — my deputy of three years, the woman who knew my coffee order and my lab entry habits and the specific angle at which I held a scalpel — had noticed behavioral anomalies. A sequence of small discrepancies — a date misremembered, a preference reversed, a microexpression slightly off in a recorded briefing — had accumulated until she had flagged it through proper Alopes channels. I had taught Lonicera to read behavioral patterns the way I read tissue samples. If she had flagged it, the deviation was substantial. Akio would confirm what Lonicera had begun.

Akio. The name surfaced on Day 58. Someone — a woman, by the voice — said: "Akio's back from the outer stations. He's the one who'll notice fastest. He knows Yukio better than anyone."

Akio. Yes. I knew Akio. My brother. Three minutes older. The youngest Marshal in the history of the Jackalope Guard — promoted at 23 over the objections of generals twice his age, the kind of appointment that required special authorization from the Chancellor and the Grand Council. He was a soldier through and through — quiet, precise, the kind of person who remembered that I took my coffee without sugar and that I always entered the lab through the east door, never the west, because the west door's proximity to the MRI unit meant a 3-second magnetic interference delay on the badge reader that I found mildly irritating. Small data points. Behavioral fingerprints. Eleven years of accumulated micro-behaviors that no training protocol could fully replicate.

Akio would notice. The woman was right. If Akio was back, the operation's window was closing.

I pressed my hand against the wall. Sublevel 2. I could hear the facility breathing — the ventilation, the power conduits, the faint electromagnetic hum of the magnetic locks. Somewhere above me, the world was collapsing MARY-07's performance. The Shire family was reviewing her. Akio was scrutinizing her. Lonicera was building a case.

And I was here, in Cell 7, with a bolt-scratched map on the underside of a desk, counting days.

### Day 68 (2343.05.22)

The detonation was not loud. It was precise — a shaped charge on the sublevel 2 blast door, calibrated to fail the magnetic lock mechanism without compromising the structural integrity of the surrounding corridor. Military engineering. Surgical.

The door of my cell swung open at 0347 — the same timestamp as my first waking in this facility, 68 days earlier. A coincidence. I chose to interpret it as symmetry.

There were four operatives in tactical gear. The lead — the one who cut my restraints and handed me a thermal blanket — had the bearing of a former Federation Navy combat medic. She spoke clipped Standard with a Meridian corporate accent: "Dr. Tanaka. We're here to extract you. Multiple confirmed hostiles neutralized. Facility is being secured. Typhi leadership has been detained."

I stood up. My legs were deconditioned — 68 days of reduced gravity environment and inadequate load-bearing exercise had reduced my quadriceps cross-sectional area by approximately 12%. I noted this clinically. I could rebuild. I was a regenerative medicine scientist.

"Dr. Yukio?" A second operative, male, pointed at the corridor. "This way."

"Wait," I said. "MARY-07."

The operative paused. He had not expected me to know the name. I watched his expression recalibrate — a micro-assessment, threat-evaluation split-second. He concluded I was not a threat.

"She's here," he said. "Facility director's office. She surrendered without resistance when the perimeter was breached."

MARY-07 was here. In this facility. Not in the world, wearing my face at Shire galas. Here, in the building, in the director's office, wearing whatever they had dressed her in when the operation collapsed.

I walked toward the director's office.

---

The corridor was exactly as I had mapped from sound: 34 meters, seven doors on the left, service junction at the midpoint, air handler in the ceiling producing the 60-decibel hum I had catalogued on Day 23. I had been right about everything. The dimensions. The layout. The zone scheduling. My mental model was accurate to within 0.4 meters.

The director's office door was open. The room was clinical — white walls, a desk, a single chair, two operatives at the door. And in the chair, facing me: a woman with my face.

She was sitting exactly as I would sit. Left hand on the right armrest. Right foot slightly forward. Head at a 7-degree inclination. She had adopted my posture from the Meridian Medical staff photography — I recognized the angle from the 2341 annual report cover shoot. She was being me, even now, even in defeat, even with my face frozen in an expression I had never practiced: fear, confusion, and something else.

I recognized it. It was the expression I had seen in the mirror at 0347 on Day 1, before I had suppressed it.

I stood in the doorway. She looked up. We looked at each other.

She was me. Epidermal layer identical. Subcutaneous adipose distribution within 2%. Stress hormone cascade visible in pupillary dynamics — I could read the physiological stress response pattern as clearly as any medical diagnostic. She was metabolically terrified. Her cortisol was spiking. Her left hand was trembling at 3 hertz — a fine motor oscillation I could calculate to approximately 0.4 millimeters of displacement.

She was alive. She was conscious. She was, in every measurable dimension, a person.

"You used my face for 68 days," I said. My voice was calm. I could hear its calmness as though from a distance — the flat affect, the measured cadence, the scientist's voice stripped of performance. "You met Vasud. You attended the Shire gala. You agreed to marry a man in my name. You stood in my lab and gave my colleagues directions."

She said nothing. Her eyes moved to my hands — looking for threat, assessing posture. I noted this. She was trained in behavioral threat assessment. She was better at reading body language than I was.

And here — here — the crack opened. Not in her. In me.

Because the truth was: I had spent 68 days training myself not to feel. I had catalogued every sensation, annotated every fear, filed away every moment of panic under the heading *data*. I had become a machine to survive. And now the machine was standing across from the woman who had worn my face into Vasud's arms — and the machine could not admit, even to itself, that what it felt was not clinical curiosity.

What I felt was this: *Vasud trusted her. Vasud trusted someone wearing my face. And I will never know if what he felt for me was real, or if it was always just the template.*

What I felt was this: *Twelve officials lost their positions. Some took their own lives. They were disgraced because of what she did with my name. And I was here counting camera sweep cycles while their careers collapsed.*

What I felt was this: *Akio came for me. He mobilized the Jackalope Guard and crossed three jurisdictions to extract a woman he thought was me — and I do not know if he would have done the same for the woman standing here now.*

These were not scientific observations. These were wounds. I had 68 days of suppressed terror to process, and I had approximately four seconds before the operative at the door would decide I needed medical attention.

I filed the wounds. I would open that file later.

"The surgical modification work is extraordinary," I said. "The behavioral conditioning depth is 11 years ahead of current published science. You are a scientific achievement."

I stepped closer. Her breathing rate increased to 18 cycles per minute — mild hyperventilation, consistent with acute stress response. Her pupils dilated further. She was calculating escape vectors — three operatives at the door, me in the immediate doorway, the window sealed with magnetic locks, no weapons visible but two operatives carrying sidearms in the hip holsters I could see through the door frame.

I stopped 1.2 meters away. The optimal conversational distance. Close enough to read micro-expressions. Far enough to maintain boundary.

"You lived my life," I said. "Not well. Not accurately. But completely enough to deceive Vasud for a month, to deceive the Shire family for three weeks, and to fool Typhi's quality assurance for 68 days. You learned fast."

She swallowed. "They trained me on you."

"Yes," I said. "They did."

I looked at her — at my own face, wearing an expression of terrified humanity that I had spent 68 days training myself not to show. She was not a weapon. She was not a tool. She was a person — surgically modified, psychologically conditioned, trained to simulate me — but a person, in every sense that the word mattered. She feared death. She feared exposure. She feared me.

"The question," I said, "is what you learned. Not what they taught you to do. What you chose to do when no one was watching. When you met Vasud and he trusted you — what did you feel? When Shire's people welcomed you into their home — did you feel anything? When you stood in my lab and people looked at you thinking you were me — did you understand what that meant?"

She was silent. Her left hand had stopped trembling. Her cortisol was stabilizing. She was making a decision — not to speak, but to choose what kind of silence this was.

I waited.

"I felt everything," she said finally. Her voice was my voice — the same pitch, the same cadence, the same Meridian accent. But the inflection was different. She had added something I had never put into my own speech pattern: warmth. A fractional slowing of consonants that conveyed empathy, connection, humanity. She had improved on my template.

I noted this. The doppelganger had exceeded the original.

"Then we have something to discuss," I said.

I turned to the operative at the door. "She's a material witness to a multi-system corporate intelligence operation spanning three jurisdictions. She will need medical evaluation, secure housing, and legal representation. She is not a prisoner. She is a victim."

MARY-07 looked at me. For the first time, her expression was not my expression. It was her own — uncertain, uncertainly human, uncertainly grateful in a way I had never seen in any mirror.

I walked out of the director's office, down the corridor I had mapped by sound, toward the extraction elevator, toward daylight, toward the 68 days that the world believed had been lived by someone else.

Behind me, the facility hummed its mechanical breath. Somewhere in the Federation, Shire family lawyers were filing injunctions. Somewhere, Akio was debriefing Meridian security. Somewhere, Vasud was learning that the woman who had promised him regulatory intelligence had been a Typhi operative — trained, modified, and deployed by Abteilung Stille, but still, at the end, a person who had felt everything.

And here, in a corridor built for secrets, the scientist who had been erased walked toward the surface, carrying two sets of data in her mind: the operation she had documented through 68 days of silence, and the one piece of information that no one — not Umin, not Typhi, not Shire — had anticipated.

MARY-07 had felt everything. Which meant she was not a proxy.

Which meant she was, in every way that mattered, my sister.

I did not yet know what to do with this information. I did not yet know what to do with any of it — with Vasud, with Shire, with the twelve officials whose careers I had indirectly destroyed, with the sister I had not asked for and did not yet know how to love.

I would learn. I had six months of psychological therapy ahead of me, according to the medical report the operative handed me on the transport. Nightmares. Trust issues. Survivor's guilt. Hyperawareness of behavioral inconsistency in others. The psychiatrist's file would list them as symptoms. I already knew them as companions.

I climbed into the transport. The doors sealed. The engine cycled.

The woman who had been erased — the scientist, the sister, the daughter, the twin, the one who could not stop asking questions — closed her eyes, and for the first time in 68 days, allowed herself to feel the full weight of everything she had survived.

It was heavier than she had calculated.
