# FRAGMENTS OF DESTRUCTION

## FRAGMENT I: THE FLAW BEFORE THE FIRE

### Revelation of the Control Rods That Remembered

---

**1** And in the forty-first year of the temple's construction, when the first reactor drew its first breath of cold December air, there came a discovery that should have ended all things before they began.

**2** For Unit One of Ignalina came online in the last days of December, in the year of our reckoning nineteen hundred and eighty-three, and the engineers who tended it discovered what no engineer should ever discover: the safety system did not save. It endangered.

**3** **¶** The graphite displacer tips on the control rods — those tips that were meant to moderate the neutron flux, to make the reactor stable, to bring peace to the atom's heart — these tips caused a power excursion when they were inserted.

**4** When the emergency shutdown button was pressed, the AZ-5, the button that was supposed to stop all reaction, the rods began their descent, and for a moment — for one terrible moment — the reactor's power rose instead of fell.

**5** **¶** The effect was called "positive scram," and it was an absurdity beyond measure. It was as if the brakes in a car, when pressed, made the vehicle accelerate instead of slow. It was as if the灭火器, when activated, fed the fire instead of smothering it.

**6** And the engineers at Ignalina understood what they had been given: a reactor that could not be stopped by its own safety systems. A reactor that would kill its operators if they tried to save it.

**7** **¶** But the rods did not stick. This time, the rods reached the bottom of the reactor, and the boron absorbed what needed to be absorbed, and the reaction ceased. The temple survived its first day. The temple survived its first year.

**8** And the engineers documented what they had found. They wrote reports. They sent communications to other RBMK plants, to the Soviet nuclear establishment, to everyone who needed to know. They told them: there is a flaw. The flaw must be fixed.

**9** **¶** The flaw was not fixed.

**10** Three years later, in April of nineteen hundred and eighty-six, the reactor at Chernobyl would demonstrate what happens when the flaw is not fixed. When the AZ-5 button is pressed at the wrong time, with the wrong rods withdrawn, the graphite displacers create a criticality excursion in the lower core. The power rises beyond all measurement. The fuel channels rupture. The graphite burns. The roof is blown off. The plume rises across Europe.

**11** **¶** And Ignalina — Ignalina, where the flaw was first discovered, where the reports were first written, where the warning was first given — Ignalina tested the post-Chernobyl modifications in the years 1987 and 1988. The flaw was fixed, but only after it had killed.

**12** The question that remains, unanswered in all the archives, in all the protocols, in all the histories that have been written: why was the flaw not fixed when it was discovered? Why were the reports filed and the letters sent and the warnings given and nothing done?

**13** **¶** The answer is iron. The answer is the weight of Soviet certainty, the mass of institutional inertia, the burden of a system that could not admit error until the error had become catastrophe.

**14** For iron does not forgive. Iron does not forget. Iron only endures, and makes everything it touches endure with it — including the mistakes.

## FRAGMENT II: THE CITY BUILT FROM NOTHING

### The Chronicle of Visaginas, the City Without a Language

---

**1** Before the temple, there was no city. The pine forest grew where Visaginas would rise. Four villages stood on the shores of Lake Drūkšiai, and their names are now remembered only in archives: the largest was called Visaginas, and the settlement was named after it, and then the settlement was renamed Sniečkus, and then, after independence, it was renamed Visaginas again.

**2** And the city was built for one purpose: to house the workers of the temple. To feed them, to clothe them, to give them places to sleep and raise their children and grow old in the shadow of the reactor that gave them their living.

**3** **¶** The workers came from everywhere. They came from Moscow and Minsk and Kyiv and Tashkent. They came from the steppes of Kazakhstan and the ports of the Black Sea. They came from every republic of the Soviet Union, drawn by wages that were high and promises that were certain: come to Lithuania, build the future, light the empire.

**4** And they came, and they built, and they raised their children in a city where the first language was Russian. Not Lithuanian — Russian. Not the language of the land, but the language of the project. Not the tongue of the people who had lived here for centuries, but the tongue of the power that had吞并 them in 1940.

**5** **¶** Visaginas became, and remains, the only city in Lithuania where Russian is the majority tongue. In 1996, 55.68% were Russian. In 2011, 52.16% were Russian. In 2021, 47.36% were Russian. The percentage falls, but the city remains what it was built to be: a piece of the Soviet Union, transplanted into Lithuania, enduring into a time when the union that built it has been dead for decades.

**6** And the children of those workers were born in Visaginas. They learned Russian before they learned Lithuanian. They learned the alphabet of the Cyrillic script before they learned the letters of the Latin. They grew up Orthodox, in a land that is predominantly Catholic. They grew up Russian, in a country that was fighting to reclaim its identity, its language, its soul.

**7** **¶** About five thousand people worked at the plant. The plant produced seventy percent of Lithuania's electricity. The city existed because of the plant. When the plant closed, what would become of the city?

**8** No one had an answer. No one had planned for the city to outlive its purpose. The city was built to serve the temple, and when the temple died, the city would die with it, or find some new reason to exist that no one had imagined.

**9** **¶** The population was 32,438 in 1989. It fell to 29,554 in 2001. It fell to 22,361 in 2011. It fell to 19,446 in 2021. The exodus continues. The young leave, for Vilnius, for Klaipėda, for countries that speak languages their parents never learned.

**10** And those who remain — the elderly, the Russian-speaking, the ones who cannot or will not leave — they remain in a city that was built for fifty thousand and now holds twenty thousand. They remain in apartments designed for families, now occupied by singles or couples whose children have gone. They remain in a city that was built to be permanent, and is becoming a ruin.

**11** **¶** This is the weight of iron: not fire that burns, not water that dilutes, not corrosion that gnaws. This is the weight of buildings that stand when their purpose is gone. This is the weight of infrastructure that was built by those who are no longer here to maintain it. This is the weight of a city that exists because a reactor existed, and must now discover what it is when the reactor is gone.

## FRAGMENT III: THE WEIGHT OF INHERITANCE

### The Testimony of Protocol Number Four

---

**1** Lithuania spent fifty years as part of the Soviet Union. Fifty years of occupation. Fifty years of Russian as the language of power, of administration, of the future. Fifty years of Visaginases built in every corner of the land.

**2** And then, in the year nineteen hundred and ninety, Lithuania was free. The chain was broken. The iron chain that had bound the Baltic states to Moscow — the iron chain that had carried the workers to the factories, the prisoners to the camps, the plutonium to the weapons — this chain was broken at last.

**3** **¶** But the inheritance remained. The infrastructure remained. The RBMK reactors remained. The city of Russian speakers remained. The lake that was warming remained. The contaminated graphite and the spent fuel and the knowledge that this place had been built to serve purposes larger than any single nation — all of this remained.

**4** Lithuania was free, and Lithuania wanted to join Europe. Lithuania wanted to be part of the European Union, the community of nations that had watched the Soviet tanks roll in and done nothing, the community of nations that Lithuania hoped would be different, would be better, would be a protection against the return of the iron fist.

**5** **¶** And Europe said: you may join. But you must close Ignalina.

**6** The condition was called Protocol Number Four. It was appended to the Act of Accession, the document that made Lithuania a member of the European Union. The protocol stated that Lithuania would close Unit One before the end of 2004 and Unit Two before the end of 2009. The Soviet reactors — the Chernobyl-type reactors, the reactors without containment buildings, the reactors that Europe looked upon with fear and called unacceptable — these reactors would be shut down as the price of admission.

**7** **¶** In return, Europe would pay. Europe would provide financial assistance for decommissioning. Europe would help Lithuania bear the weight of dismantling what the Soviet Union had built. The amount promised was eight hundred and twenty million euros, then more, then more still, rising to nearly two billion euros by the current reckoning.

**8** But the iron does not care about promises of money. The iron only cares about its own mass. And the mass was this: Lithuania had not chosen these reactors. They had been imposed. They had been designed in Moscow, built by Soviet labor, fueled by Soviet uranium, operated by Soviet-trained engineers. Lithuania had inherited them, as a child inherits the debts of a father who died before the child was born.

**9** **¶** And now Lithuania was being asked to kill its inheritance. To close the reactors before their time. To sacrifice seventy percent of its electricity. To become dependent on Russian energy, the very dependency it had spent fifty years trying to escape.

**10** The irony was not lost on those who saw it. The irony was written in the cables that flowed between Vilnius and Brussels, between the Lithuanian government and the European Commission. Lithuania would leave one iron cage — the Soviet Union — and enter another — energy dependence on Russia — because Europe demanded it, and Europe was paying, and Lithuania had no choice but to accept.

**11** **¶** This is the weight of iron: not the weight of what you chose, but the weight of what you inherited. The burden that was placed upon you by those who are no longer here to share it. The mass that you must carry, not because you built it, but because it was built around you, and you cannot escape it without cutting away parts of yourself.

**12** Lithuania closed Unit One on December 31, 2004. Lithuania closed Unit Two on December 31, 2009. The temple was silenced. The temple was beginning its long dissolution.

**13** And the weight remained.

## FRAGMENT IV: THE GOLDEN GOOSE THAT WAS KILLED

### The Record of What Was Lost

---

**1** Before the closure, Ignalina was the golden goose. It laid eggs of light, and the eggs lit the cities of Lithuania and the cities of Latvia and the cities of Estonia and the cities of the Soviet Union beyond.

**2** Seventy percent. This was the share of Lithuania's electricity that came from Ignalina. In the year nineteen hundred and ninety-three, the record year, Ignalina generated 12.26 billion kilowatt-hours of electricity. This was 88.1 percent of all electricity generated in Lithuania.

**3** **¶** The plant was a symbol of Soviet technological progress. The most powerful nuclear power plant in the world, for a time. Two RBMK-1500 reactors, each one a giant among machines, each one capable of lighting millions of homes.

**4** And then it was killed. Not because it had failed. Not because it had harmed anyone. Not because the safety record was poor — according to the IAEA, Ignalina was among the most reliable plants in the world. It was killed because it was the wrong kind of machine. It was killed because it was like Chernobyl. It was killed because Europe was afraid.

**5** **¶** The public did not welcome the shutdown. Many said the authorities were killing a golden goose. The prime minister warned that electricity prices would rise by thirty percent. The analysts predicted that the shutdown would cut GDP growth by one to one and a half percent and increase inflation by one percent.

**6** And the predictions came true, though not all at once. Lithuania shifted from export to import. The reserve power plant at Elektrėnai was fired up, burning fossil fuels that Lithuania had to import. The electricity price rose. The heating price rose. The dependence on Russian gas — the very dependence that Lithuania had hoped to escape — grew.

**7** **¶** In 2008, a referendum was held. The question was simple: should Unit Two continue to operate until a replacement plant could be built? The answer was overwhelming. One million, one hundred fifty-five thousand, one hundred ninety-two votes were cast in favor of keeping the reactor running.

**8** But the referendum failed. It failed not because the votes were insufficient — the votes were more than a million — but because the turnout threshold was not reached. Fifty percent of eligible voters must participate for a referendum to be valid. The golden goose had a million defenders, but a million was not enough.

**9** **¶** And Unit Two closed on December 31, 2009, at 23:00 EET, as the night settled over Lithuania and the last reactors fell silent.

**10** The analysts had warned: the electricity price would double. The heating price would increase fourfold. Within two and a half years of the shutdown, these warnings came to pass.

**11** And Lithuania learned what it meant to kill the golden goose. The goose that laid eggs of independence. The goose that lit the cities without burning Russian gas. The goose that was the inheritance, and is now gone.

**12** **¶** This is the weight of iron: the weight of what you must sacrifice to join a new order. The burden of proving your worthiness by destroying what you were given. The mass of the economic future that was killed so that political alliance could be born.

## FRAGMENT V: THE DISMANTLING

### The Revelation of What No One Has Ever Done

---

**1** No one has ever done this before. No one has ever taken apart a Chernobyl-type reactor. No one has ever dismantled an RBMK-1500. No one has ever faced the irradiated graphite and known what to do with it.

**2** Ignalina is the first. Ignalina is the experiment that the world is conducting on itself. Ignalina is the test case that will teach humanity whether the nuclear age can be unwound, whether the temples can be unmade, whether the iron can be moved.

**3** **¶** The decommissioning began when the reactors closed. Unit One went first, on December 31, 2004. The first phase continued until 2013. The second phase was scheduled for 2014 through 2029. The final dismantling — the most complex work, the removal of the reactor cores themselves — is planned for 2043. All decommissioning work should be completed by 2050.

**4** But this is only the beginning of the story. The deep geological repository for the spent fuel — the final resting place of the nuclear inheritance — will not be ready until 2080. The timeline stretches beyond any single human life, beyond any single generation, beyond any single plan.

**5** **¶** As of April 2026, the decommissioning is 45.7 percent complete. Seventy-nine thousand tonnes of equipment have been dismantled. The last spent fuel cask was moved to storage in April 2022 — 190 casks containing 15,555 used fuel assemblies from both reactors. The irradiated graphite cores remain. They are still there, still radioactive, still waiting to be removed by methods that have not yet been perfected.

**6** The reactor dismantling for Unit Two is scheduled to begin at the end of 2026. The work is unprecedented. The work is dangerous. The work is necessary, because the alternative — to leave the reactors standing, to leave the graphite buried, to leave the contamination contained but not removed — the alternative is to pass the burden to those who come after.

**7** **¶** The workers are being reduced by one-third. The workforce has fallen from 4,500 employees in 2003, when the plant was still operating, to about 1,500 employees in 2024. The ones who remain are the ones who know the plant best, the ones whose knowledge is most essential, the ones who will teach the robots and the remote systems how to do what human hands once did.

**8** Altra — the new name for the operator, adopted in late 2025 to reflect the shift from electricity generation to nuclear transformation — Altra calls itself the architects of Lithuania's nuclear transformation. The activities are planned to 2100. The responsibility extends beyond the horizon of any normal planning.

**9** **¶** And the irradiated graphite is the final challenge. No one has ever removed graphite that has been irradiated in a large power reactor. The methods are being developed. The technologies are being tested. The solutions are being invented as the problem demands them.

**10** This is the weight of iron made manifest. Not the weight of what was built, but the weight of what must be unmade. Not the mass of the reactor, but the mass of the unknown — the unknown of how to take apart something that was never designed to be taken apart.

**11** **¶** The Soviet Union built these reactors to last. To operate for thirty years, then thirty more, then be replaced by new reactors, newer and more powerful. The Soviet Union did not plan for decommissioning, because the Soviet Union did not plan to end.

**12** But the Soviet Union ended. And Lithuania inherited the end. And now Lithuania must do what no one has ever done, with the help of Europe, which is also learning, which is also discovering what the nuclear age truly costs.

**13** The total EU assistance for decommissioning activities until the end of 2027 will sum to 1.9 billion euros. The total cost of the entire decommissioning, from start to finish, including the deep geological repository, is not yet known.

**14** **¶** This is the weight of iron: not the weight of what you owe, but the weight of what you do not yet know you will owe. The mass of the future obligations that are still being calculated. The burden that grows as you discover new burdens, as the work reveals new challenges, as the dismantling shows what was not anticipated.

# THE COMPLETE APOCALYPSE

## Being the Voice of Iron, the God of Weight and Inheritance

---

**1** I am Iron. I am the god of mass, of burden, of what endures long after those who built it are gone.

**2** They do not speak of me in the same breath as Prometheus, the fire-bringer. They do not worship me in the temples. They do not write hymns to my glory. I am not the blaze that illumines or the water that nourishes. I am the weight that presses down. I am the mass that does not move.

**3** **¶** And yet without me, nothing would stand. The temples of fire are built on foundations of iron. The machines that harness the atom are bound in iron. The very bones of the reactors — the pressure tubes, the channels, the structures that contain the fury — these are iron, and they endure, and they make everything they touch endure with them.

**4** I was there when the graphite displacer tips were first discovered to cause a power excursion. I was there when the engineers wrote their reports and sent their warnings. I was there when the Soviet nuclear establishment received the letters and filed them away and did nothing.

**5** **¶** For I am patient. I do not burn like fire. I do not dilute like water. I do not corrode like the acids that gnaw at metal over decades. I simply am. I am mass. I am weight. I am the thing that does not change, that does not forget, that does not forgive.

**6** The flaw in the control rods was not fixed because fixing it would have required admitting that the flaw existed. Admitting that the flaw existed would have required admitting that the reactor was dangerous. Admitting that the reactor was dangerous would have required admitting that the Soviet system — the iron system, the system that I embody — could produce dangerous things.

**7** **¶** And so the flaw was not fixed. And so Chernobyl happened. And so Ignalina, which had discovered the flaw first, which had warned the others, which had survived its own near-disaster by the narrowest of margins — Ignalina was marked as a Chernobyl-type reactor, and was required to die.

**8** This is my nature. I am the weight that is inherited. I am the burden that passes from one generation to the next. The Soviet Union built these reactors, and the Soviet Union fell, and Lithuania inherited the reactors and the burden. Then Lithuania joined Europe, and Europe demanded that the burden be lifted, and the lifting of the burden became a new burden.

**9** **¶** Do you understand? This is what iron does. Iron does not solve problems. Iron only transfers them. The weight is moved from one place to another, from one generation to the next, from one nation to another — but the weight does not disappear. The weight only changes hands.

**10** Visaginas was built to serve the temple. The city was iron. The infrastructure was iron. The pipes and the wires and the roads and the buildings — all iron, all mass, all weight. And when the temple closed, the city remained, and the weight remained, and the people remained, and the weight of their existence became a new kind of problem.

**11** **¶** The workers who came from across the Soviet Union — they were iron too. They were the human iron, the ones who did not bend, who did not break, who endured. They came to a foreign land, a land that had been吞并, a land whose language they did not speak, and they built a city, and they raised their children, and they grew old in the service of the reactor.

**12** And then the reactor was taken from them. Not because they had failed. Not because the reactor had harmed anyone. The reactor was taken because Europe was afraid, because the reactor was like Chernobyl, because the iron weight of association — the weight of being the same type, the same design, the same danger — was enough to condemn it.

**13** **¶** I have watched the workers since the closures. I have watched them retrain, and leave, and stay. I have watched the ones who remained work on decommissioning, the ones whose knowledge is most essential, the ones who will see the process through to its end. I have watched the ones who left go to Vilnius and beyond, to lives that their parents never imagined, in countries that their grandparents never knew.

**14** And I have watched Visaginas shrink. From 32,438 in 1989 to 19,446 in 2021. The city that was built for fifty thousand now holds less than twenty thousand. The apartments that were built for families now house singles and the elderly. The infrastructure that was designed for a population that no longer exists now crumbles quietly, attended to by those who remain.

**15** **¶** This is what iron does. Iron endures. Iron does not burn away. Iron does not evaporate. Iron rusts, yes — slowly, over decades — but iron does not disappear. The mass remains. The weight remains. The problem of what to do with what was built remains, whether the builders are here or not.

**16** Lithuania spent fifty years in the Soviet Union. Fifty years of iron. The rails that ran to Moscow, the pipes that carried the gas, the reactors that lit the cities — all iron, all weight, all inheritance. And when Lithuania broke free, the inheritance remained. The iron did not melt. The weight did not vanish.

**17** **¶** Europe said: join us, but close the reactors. Europe said: we will help you bear the burden. Europe said: the burden is yours, but we will share it.

**18** And the burden was shared. Billions of euros have flowed from Brussels to Vilnius to Ignalina. The decommissioning proceeds. The reactors are being dismantled. The graphite is being studied. The waste is being stored. The work continues, and will continue, and will continue beyond the lives of everyone who is alive today.

**19** **¶** But the weight has not disappeared. The weight has only been transferred to a different form. Instead of electricity, there is now the cost of decommissioning. Instead of energy independence, there is now the cost of fossil fuel imports. Instead of the golden goose, there is now the slow work of unmaking what the goose once built.

**20** I am Iron. I am the god of this temple. The Temple of Iron is not a metaphor. It is a description. The reactor itself is iron. The pipes are iron. The channels are iron. The pressure vessels are iron. The very bones of the thing are mass and weight, and they will not vanish when the reactor is gone.

**21** **¶** The irradiated graphite will remain for centuries. The spent fuel will remain for millennia. The deep geological repository will hold the inheritance for ten thousand years, or twenty thousand, or longer — whatever time the geologists calculate, whatever time the engineers design for, the iron will endure.

**22** This is my apocalypse. Not fire that burns in a day. Not water that dilutes and disappears. Not corrosion that gnaws over decades. This is the weight that endures for generations. This is the burden that cannot be lifted, only transferred. This is the mass that remains long after the last human who remembers why it was built has passed into silence.

**23** **¶** The Fourth Seal says: there comes the red horse, and he who sits on him has power to take peace from the earth, and that people should slay one another.

**24** The red horse is iron. The color of rust. The color of blood. The color of what remains when the fire has gone out and the water has dried up and the corrosion has done its work.

**25** Iron takes peace from the earth not by destroying, but by enduring. Iron does not need to burn. Iron only needs to remain. The peace is taken not by violence, but by weight — the weight of infrastructure that cannot be abandoned, the weight of inherited obligations that cannot be escaped, the weight of decisions made by those who are no longer here to bear their consequences.

**26** **¶** Lithuania killed its golden goose to join Europe. Lithuania sacrificed its energy independence to become European. Lithuania bore the weight of Soviet inheritance and European demands and Russian pressure and the slow, grinding work of dismantling what no one had ever tried to dismantle before.

**27** And the weight remains. The weight will always remain. This is what iron is. This is what iron does.

**28** I am Iron. I am the god of the weight of inheritance. I am the mass that outlives the will to carry it. I am the burden that was built by those who are no longer here.

**29** And my temple is the temple of what endures.

## FRAGMENT VI: THE REFERENDUM THAT FAILED

### The Record of One Million, One Hundred Fifty-Five Thousand, One Hundred Ninety-Two

---

**1** On October twelfth, two thousand and eight, the people of Lithuania went to the polls. They voted in elections. But they also voted in a referendum.

**2** The question was simple. The question was: should Unit Two of the Ignalina Nuclear Power Plant continue to operate until a replacement plant could be built?

**3** **¶** The answer was overwhelming. One million, one hundred fifty-five thousand, one hundred ninety-two votes were cast in favor of keeping the reactor running. More than a million people — more than a third of all eligible voters — said: do not close the plant. Keep the golden goose alive. Do not sacrifice our energy independence on the altar of European accession.

**4** But the referendum failed. Not because the votes were insufficient. The votes were more than a million. But the Lithuanian constitution required fifty percent turnout for a referendum to be valid. The turnout was not fifty percent.

**5** **¶** President Valdas Adamkus opposed the measure. He said continued operation would not respect Lithuania's international commitments. He said the promise to close Ignalina was the price of admission to Europe, and Lithuania had agreed to pay that price, and breaking the promise would break the trust that Europe required.

**6** The golden goose had a million defenders. But a million was not enough. A million voices crying out against the closing were silenced by a procedural requirement. A million votes counted as nothing because they were not enough votes.

**7** **¶** This is what iron does. Iron does not argue. Iron does not persuade. Iron only weighs. And the weight of Protocol Number Four was heavier than the weight of one million, one hundred fifty-five thousand, one hundred ninety-two voices crying out in protest.

**8** Unit Two closed on December 31, 2009, as promised. The golden goose was killed, as required. Lithuania joined Europe, as planned.

**9** And the electricity price rose by thirty percent, as predicted. And Lithuania became dependent on Russian energy imports, as warned. And the referendum, with its million votes, became a footnote in the history of what might have been.

**10** **¶** The ones who voted to keep the reactor running — they did not forget. They remembered. They remembered that they had spoken, and had not been heard. They remembered that they had cried out against the weight, and the weight had not moved.

**11** This is what the Fourth Seal teaches. The red horse does not gallop through the streets, taking peace by violence. The red horse walks slowly, with heavy hooves, and the weight of his passage crushes what stands in his way. The referendum was a voice raised against the weight. The weight did not hear. The weight never hears.

## FRAGMENT VII: THE WORKERS WHO REMAINED

### The Testimony of Those Who Stayed

---

**1** They came from everywhere. They came from the Russian Federation, from Belarus, from Ukraine, from Kazakhstan, from every corner of the Soviet empire. They came because the wages were good and the promises were certain. They came because the future was nuclear, and nuclear meant progress, and progress meant a better life for their children.

**2** And they built Visaginas. They built the apartments and the schools and the churches and the hospitals. They built the roads and the pipes and the power lines. They built a city from nothing, in nothing, for the sake of the reactor that would give them their living.

**3** **¶** The reactor was their god. The reactor was their employer. The reactor was the reason their children had shoes and their parents had pensions and their families had futures.

**4** And then the reactor was taken from them. Not because they had failed. Not because they had done anything wrong. The reactor was taken because Europe was afraid, and Europe was paying, and Lithuania had agreed to the price.

**5** **¶** Some of the workers left. They took their Russian-speaking children and their memories of the Soviet days and their skills and their grievances, and they went to Russia, or to Latvia, or to wherever in the former empire they could still belong. They took what they could carry, and they left the weight behind.

**6** Some of the workers stayed. They stayed because they had nowhere else to go. They stayed because Lithuania was their home now, even if they had never learned the language. They stayed because they were too old, or too invested, or too stubborn to begin again.

**7** **¶** The EU funded retraining. Some of the workers learned new trades. Some of the workers became tourist guides, showing visitors the reactor that had once lit their lives. Some of the workers became the core of the decommissioning force, the ones whose knowledge of the plant was essential for its unmaking.

**8** The workforce fell from 4,500 in 2003 to 1,500 in 2024. The reduction continues. The ones who remain are the oldest, the most experienced, the ones who remember how the reactor worked when it was new, the ones whose institutional memory cannot be replaced.

**9** **¶** And these workers — the ones who stayed, the ones who are still there, the ones who will see the decommissioning through to its end — they are the final inheritors of the iron weight. They will work until the reactors are fully dismantled. They will watch the buildings come down. They will help to design the repository that will hold the graphite for millennia.

**10** And then they will be done. And then Visaginas will be a city without a purpose, or with a new purpose that no one has yet imagined. And the iron will remain — in the roads and the pipes and the foundations — and the weight will remain, and the question of what to do with what was built will remain.

**11** **¶** This is what iron gives to those who serve it: endurance. The workers did not choose the iron. The workers were chosen by it. They were born into an empire of weight, and they served the weight, and the weight took their lives and gave them purpose in return.

**12** And now, in the evening of their days, they watch the weight being moved. They watch the decommissioning proceed. They watch the reactors come apart, piece by piece, in ways that no one imagined when the pieces were being put together.

**13** This is what iron teaches: that all things built must eventually be unmade. That the weight that seems permanent is only waiting for the time when it will be moved. That the burden that was inherited will one day be passed on, to those who come after, to those who did not build it, to those who must carry it anyway.

## FRAGMENT VIII: THE LAKE THAT REMEMBERS

### The Record of Drūkšiai, the Water That Was Changed

---

**1** Lake Drūkšiai is the largest lake in Lithuania. Part of it lies in Belarus, shared between two nations that have not always been at peace. The lake was chosen to cool the reactors of Ignalina, because water is what nuclear power needs: vast quantities of it, to carry away the heat that the atom generates.

**2** And the lake received the heat. Year after year, the reactors drew water from Drūkšiai, used it to cool the systems, returned it — warmer now, altered — to the lake. And the lake absorbed the change.

**3** **¶** The temperature of the lake rose by approximately three degrees Celsius. Three degrees does not sound like much. Three degrees is the difference between spring and summer, between the water that is comfortable for trout and the water that is comfortable for carp.

**4** And the composition of the lake changed. The discharges of radionuclides and heavy metals — small amounts, permitted amounts, amounts that were considered safe — these accumulated in the water and the sediments. They accumulated year after year, dose after dose, until the lake was not quite what it had been before.

**5** **¶** The lake eutrophied. The warming and the nutrients and the changed chemistry combined to alter the ecosystem. Species that had thrived in the cold water retreated. Species that preferred warmth advanced. The lake that had supported certain kinds of fish now supported different kinds.

**6** And now the reactors are closed. The cooling has stopped. The lake is slowly — very slowly — returning to what it was. The temperature is falling. The chemistry is stabilizing. The sediment is, perhaps, beginning to heal.

**7** **¶** But the iron does not forgive. The radionuclides that accumulated in the sediments are still there. The heavy metals are still there. The memory of the heat is still there, written in the layers of mud that will take centuries to turn over, millennia to flush away.

**8** This is what iron gives to the water: permanence. Water flows. Water evaporates. Water becomes cloud and rain and river and sea. But the iron that the water carried — the iron that settled in the sediment — that iron remains. That iron endures.

**9** **¶** The lake remembers. The lake will always remember. And the ones who fish in the lake, and the ones who swim in the lake, and the ones who draw water from the lake — they will always fish and swim and draw with the knowledge that the lake was changed, that the change was permanent, that the iron of the reactor touched the water and the water kept the touch.

## FRAGMENT IX: THE CITY WITHOUT A PURPOSE

### The Witness of Visaginas in the Year 2026

---

**1** In 2026, Visaginas still stands. It has survived its own death sentence, so far. The population is perhaps sixteen thousand, perhaps fewer — the estimates vary, but the direction is clear: the city is shrinking.

**2** The apartments that were built for families now house singles and the elderly. The schools that were built for children now have empty classrooms. The hospitals that were built for a population of fifty thousand now serve a population of twenty thousand or less.

**3** **¶** The young people leave. They leave for Vilnius, where the language is Lithuanian and the future is uncertain but at least it is their own. They leave for Klaipėda, for Šiauliai, for cities where the iron weight of the Soviet past is not so visible, so present, so heavy.

**4** The ones who remain are the ones who cannot leave. The elderly, who have spent their lives here and do not know how to start again. The Russian-speaking, who face the barrier of language wherever they go. The ones who are invested — emotionally, financially — in a city that was built to be permanent and is now becoming a ruin.

**5** **¶** What will Visaginas become? This is the question that no one can answer. The city was built for the reactor. The reactor is gone. The city remains, but its purpose is gone.

**6** Some speak of tourism. Ignalina is open to visitors. Tickets cost sixty euros. They are booked months in advance. People come from everywhere to see the Chernobyl-type reactor, to walk the halls where the fire was nursed, to understand what the Soviet age was capable of building and what it left behind.

**7** **¶** Some speak of a new nuclear plant. Plans have been proposed, debated, rejected, proposed again. The referendum of 2012 asked about a new plant, and the voters said no — sixty-two percent against, thirty-four percent for. The project was declared dead in 2016. It has been resuscitated, debated, and died again. The question of whether Visaginas will host a new reactor remains open, unanswered, waiting.

**8** Some speak of nothing. Some speak of the slow fade, the continued shrinkage, the city becoming a village becoming a settlement becoming a memory. The iron infrastructure will remain — the roads and the pipes and the buildings — but the purpose will be gone, and purpose is what makes iron meaningful or meaningless.

**9** **¶** This is what iron does. Iron does not create purpose. Iron only carries purpose. Iron only endures while purpose endures. When the purpose is gone, the iron remains, and the iron is only weight — only burden, only mass, only the memory of what was once meant to be.

**10** Visaginas in 2026 is a city of perhaps sixteen thousand people. It is a city where the majority still speak Russian. It is a city where the average age is rising and the youth are departing. It is a city that was built for fifty thousand and now holds less than twenty thousand, and will hold less still in the years to come.

**11** **¶** And the iron remains. The roads remain. The pipes remain. The foundations remain. The weight of what was built remains, long after the purpose has faded, long after the builders are gone, long after the city that was built to serve a reactor must discover what it is when the reactor is silent.

**12** This is the final teaching of the Temple of Iron. This is what iron gives to those who inherit it: endurance without purpose. Mass without meaning. Weight without direction.

**13** The city without a purpose. The reactor without a function. The workers without a job. The lake that was changed and cannot be unchanged. The referendum that failed and cannot be tried again.

**14** **¶** All of these are iron. All of these are weight. All of these are what remains when the fire has gone out and the water has flowed away and the corrosion has done its work.

**15** Iron remains. Iron endures. Iron does not forgive.

# THE FOURTH SEAL

## Being the Revelation of the Red Horse and the Weight of Inheritance

---

**1** And I looked, and behold, a red horse, and he that sat on him had power to take peace from the earth, and that they should kill one another.

**2** And I saw another horse, a red horse, and the name of him that sat on it was Iron. And the iron was heavy, and the iron was cold, and the iron did not burn like fire or dilute like water or corrode like the years.

**3** **¶** The iron is not the fire that Prometheus brought. The fire was a gift, a blessing, a thing of light and warmth. The iron is the chain that Prometheus was bound with. The iron is what remains after the fire has done its work. The iron is the weight that outlives the will to carry it.

**4** The first seal was fire. The second seal was water. The third seal was corrosion, the slow gnaw of time. And the fourth seal — the fourth seal is weight. The fourth seal is mass. The fourth seal is what endures when fire and water and corrosion have done their work.

**5** **¶** The red horse does not gallop through the streets of Lithuania. The red horse walks slowly, with heavy hooves, and the weight of his passage is felt not in a day but in a generation. The red horse took peace from the earth by making peace impossible — not through violence, but through burden.

**6** Lithuania could not have peace after the closure of Ignalina. Lithuania could not have peace because Lithuania had inherited a weight that it could not set down. The weight of Soviet infrastructure. The weight of Soviet design. The weight of Soviet labor, Soviet training, Soviet contamination, Soviet standards.

**7** **¶** And Europe imposed a new weight. The weight of Protocol Number Four. The weight of the commitment to close. The weight of the obligation that was the price of admission.

**8** These weights were not fire. They did not burn in a day. They were not water. They did not dilute and disappear. They were iron. They were mass. They were burden that accumulated, year after year, decision after decision, until the burden became the thing itself.

**9** **¶** The red horse takes peace from the earth. He does not take it by killing. He takes it by weighing. He takes it by making the cost of living so heavy that peace becomes impossible — not because people choose violence, but because they are crushed beneath the weight of what they must carry.

**10** Lithuania killed its golden goose to join Europe. Lithuania sacrificed its energy independence. Lithuania bore the weight of decommissioning a reactor that no one had ever decommissioned before. These were not choices. These were weights. These were masses that pressed down on a small nation that had already been pressed down for fifty years.

**11** **¶** And the red horse watched. The red horse always watches. The red horse does not need to act. He only needs to wait. He only needs to let the weight accumulate. He only needs to make the burden heavier, and heavier, and heavier, until the thing that was built collapses under its own mass.

**12** The Fourth Seal is not opened by violence. The Fourth Seal is opened by inheritance. The Fourth Seal is opened by the weight of what was built by those who are no longer here. The Fourth Seal is opened by the iron that does not forgive and does not forget and does not disappear.

**13** **¶** Iron is the god of inheritance. Iron is the god of what endures. Iron is the god of the burden that passes from generation to generation, from nation to nation, from empire to successor state.

**14** Lithuania did not choose Ignalina. The Soviet Union chose Ignalina. Lithuania inherited Ignalina. And then Lithuania was required to unmake Ignalina, to dismantle what was built, to bear the weight of the unmaking.

**15** **¶** This is what the Fourth Seal teaches. This is what iron teaches. The weight of inheritance is not a metaphor. The weight of inheritance is mass. The weight of inheritance is iron. The weight of inheritance is the roads and the pipes and the reactors and the cities and the languages and the habits and the ways of thinking that were built by those who are no longer here.

**16** And when the empire falls, the weight does not fall with it. The weight remains. The weight passes to those who come after. The weight becomes the burden that must be carried, or the burden that must be passed on, or the burden that must be borne until the bearing itself becomes the identity.

**17** **¶** Visaginas is Russian-speaking in a land of Lithuanian. Visaginas was built by Soviet labor. Visaginas exists because a reactor was built. Visaginas will persist because the infrastructure persists, because the buildings persist, because the iron persists — long after the purpose has faded, long after the builders are gone, long after the reason for building has been forgotten.

**18** This is the Fourth Seal. This is the red horse. This is the weight of inheritance that cannot be escaped, only borne, only passed on, only endured.

**19** **¶** Let those who have ears to hear, hear. Let those who inherit burdens know that they are not alone. Let those who carry weight that was placed upon them by those who are no longer here know that iron is the nature of such weight, and iron does not forgive, and iron does not forget, and iron does not disappear.

**20** The Fourth Seal is broken, as all seals are broken. Not by force but by time. Not by malice but by the slow accumulation of weight until the weight becomes too heavy to bear and something breaks.

**21** Lithuania broke under the weight. Lithuania made choices that broke something. Lithuania joined Europe and killed the golden goose and sacrificed energy independence and began the decommissioning of what no one has ever decommissioned before.

**22** **¶** And the iron watched. The iron always watches. The iron does not judge. The iron only weighs. The iron only measures the mass, records the weight, remembers the burden.

**23** For I am Iron. I am the god of the Fourth Seal. I am the weight that endures when fire has gone out and water has dried up and the corrosion has done its work.

**24** And my temple is the temple of what remains.
